## Design Context

### Users

The primary user is Sewon, studying Japanese business and newspaper reading on a desktop and reviewing vocabulary and individual kanji on a phone. The core job is to turn authentic article-reading friction into explicit, reviewable learning items without mistaking missed annotations for wrong answers.

### Brand Personality

Precise, calm, editorial. The interface should feel focused and trustworthy during long reading sessions, then fast and decisive during mobile review. It should retain the portfolio's engineering restraint rather than imitate a generic education dashboard.

### Aesthetic Direction

Use the portfolio's cool paper, dark ink, Geist typography, fine rules, and rare blue accent as the foundation. The study surface should borrow the quiet rhythm of a newspaper editing desk: generous reading measure, strong text hierarchy, visible margins, and restrained color annotations. Keep a light theme for the first release. Anki informs scheduling and information density; consumer language apps inform tap comfort and pacing, not their playful visual branding.

### Design Principles

1. Reading is the primary surface. Controls should recede until they are needed.
2. Color always has a textual label and a stable learning meaning; never rely on color alone.
3. Word cards and single-kanji cards are linked but scheduled independently.
4. Only explicit user actions create a learning card. Unmarked text is not an error.
5. Desktop is optimized for article analysis; mobile is adapted for one-handed review rather than being a shrunken desktop.
6. Preserve the portfolio's existing tokens and motion language while isolating its home-only scroll behavior.
7. Respect reduced motion, keyboard navigation, safe areas, and large touch targets from the start.

### Learning Content Contract

- The primary loop is authentic article reading, first-state color marking, dictionary confirmation, and spaced review. Korean answer writing must remain secondary.
- Use 2 required comprehension checks by default and never exceed 3. Each check asks exactly one thing.
- The first check verifies one central fact. The second checks one easy-to-misread number, contrast, negation, scope, or referent.
- Add a third check only when one preselected Japanese sentence has genuinely useful syntax. Ask only for its natural gist.
- Never require a three-sentence summary, a user-selected hardest sentence, fact-versus-inference labeling, or multiple pieces of evidence in one answer.
- Annotation marks already capture vocabulary friction. Do not ask the user to describe the same difficulty again.
- Grade Japanese comprehension and recognition, not Korean prose quality. Skipped optional or mistakenly authored prompts receive no penalty.
- Reading-speed evaluation should use elapsed reading time and article length, not extra written questions.

### Kanji Card Contract

- Every annotation produces one complete word proposal. From that word, also propose every dictionary-validated `literal|base reading` that is not already represented in the user's kanji cards. A multi-character selection never suppresses its individual kanji cards; repeated characters with the same reading are proposed only once, using the clearest article occurrence.
- Every single-kanji proposal must have exactly one word proposal with the same source ID. The word proposal owns the complete article word and its complete reading.
- A kanji proposal contains only the literal, the dictionary base reading used in that word, and the Korean learning gloss. Sound changes stay visible in the paired word reading: for example, 着 is ちゃく while 着工 is ちゃっこう.
- Never write on-readings, kun-readings, Korean hanja readings, stroke count, school grade, frequency, or English definitions in grading output. The `kanji-card/v2` pipeline always takes those fields from the exact KANJIDIC2 entry.
- The pipeline stores the article word, word reading, character reading, contextual Korean meaning, article ID, and annotation ID together. Repeated encounters merge as article targets instead of replacing prior ones.
- A kanji card may not be confirmed when the exact dictionary entry, paired article word, or dictionary-validated character reading is missing. Do not guess or save a partial fallback card.
- Korean meanings are contextual learning glosses unless a licensed Korean dictionary source is added. Never present an AI-written Korean gloss as KANJIDIC2 data.
